### After Virgil names some of the historical figures…
“Poschia ch’io ebbi ‘l mio dottore udito nomar le donne antiche e’ cavalieri, pieta mi giunse, e fui quasi smarrito” (Dante V, 70-72)

“No sooner had I heard my teacher name the ancient ladies and the knights, than pity seized me and I was like a man astray. My first words: ‘Poet, I should willingly speak with <b><i><u>those two who go together there and seem so lightly carried by the wind</u></i></b>.’” (Mandelbaum V, 70-75)

“When he had finished allocating blame to all those ancient dames and cavaliers, a rush of pity caused me to exclaim: ‘I fain would to that twosome speak, who steer so lightly by the starless wind, they seem a ghostly gondola and gondolier’” (Carson V, 70-75)

The pair whom Dante calls out too are linked closely, when all of the other historical figures named (Dido, Cleopatra, Helen, Achilles, Paris, Tristan) are named as individuals. Helen and Paris, the only other pair who even *could* be considered jointly, are separated by Achilles in a clearly deliberate (and frankly delightfully winking ) way. They are intertwined at an essential level, which Dante reflects in his presentation of them as they leave the course and approach him. As Lucan introduced Cato, “like in a fruitful field a lofty oak, bearing the people’s spoils of old and generals’ hallowed dedications; clinging with roots no longer strong, by its own weight it stands firm, and spreading naked branches through the air, it makes shade with trunk, not foliage; and though it totters, ready to fall beneath the first Eurus, though all around so many trees upraise themselves with sturdy trunks, yet it alone is venerated”, Dante continues the classical tradition of deep natural simile with his introduction of Francesca and her lover (Lucan I, 135-142). “Even as doves when summoned by desire, borne forward by their will, move through the air with wings uplifted, still, to their sweet nest, those spirits left the ranks where Dido suffers, approaching us through the malignant air” (Mandelbaum V, 82-86). By taking a pre-Christian tradition of nature-based characterization and blending it with an image laden with Christian meaning, Dante here leaves us with a microcosmic echo of his entire effort. The paired, romantic essence of the doves is spelled out even more plainly in Carson, who takes some liberties with the verse, saying “when, summoned by desire, they swoop into their nest like loving groom and bride” (Carson V, 83-84). All of this work is done ahead of Francesca’s first words to establish a clear image in the mind of Dante the traveler and the reader themselves: not all in this circle are criminals of the same pedigree, and even in the midst of torment, the souls about to speak move with love that is plain to see. 

To reiterate the historical context of these souls: Francesca da Rimini and her lover, Paolo Malatesta, were murdered by Giovanni Malatesta, Francesca’s husband and Paolo’s brother, after he discovered they were having an affair. Dante spent the last years of his life in exile in Ravenna, which “lies on that shore to which the Po together with the waters that follow it descends to final rest” where he was hosted by Francesca’s nephew Guido II da Polenta. When Francesca begins to tell her story, she opens with a crucial bit of anaphora. “**Love**, that can quickly seize the gentle heart, took hold of him because of the fair body taken from me— how that was done still wounds me. **Love**, that releases no beloved from loving, took hold of me so strongly through his beauty that, as you see, it has not left me yet. **Love** led the two of us unto one death” (Mandelbaum V, 100-106). Dante is immediately struck by this declaration, admitting that “I bent my head and held it low until the poet asked of me: ‘What are you thinking?’” (V 108-111).  As an aside, the dynamic of that moment is carried to more modern literature. When Scrooge is taken to see other Christmas celebrations, the ghost of Christmas present is often forced to prod him to share his internal struggles aloud. The awkward silence of strong emotion, followed by a polite “ahem” from a host, transcends centuries and cultures. 	

When Dante finally does speak, he is filled with pity. “Alas, how many gentle thoughts, how deep a longing, had led them to the agonizing pass!” (Mandelbaum V, 112-114). One of the great magic tricks of the *Divine Comedy* is Dante’s separation of his own selves: Author-Dante and Traveler-Dante. It’s a trick aided by his use of his own persona. If Dante is following Virgil through the Inferno, then he can’t be *also* writing the rules of the Inferno! What we’re reading are merely his observations, not his own designs, right? Watch both hands move, though, and the illusion fails. Traveler-Dante pities Francesca, but Author-Dante put her in Hell. 

Francesca continues on, relating how she and her lover read the story of Lancelot and the text swayed them into adultery. “Time and time again that reading lead our eyes to meet, and made our faces pale, and yet one point alone defeated us. When we had read how the desired smile was kissed by one who was so true a lover, this one, who never shall be parted from me, while all his body trembled, kissed my mouth” (Mandelbaum V, 130-136).
