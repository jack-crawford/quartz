These are all lamentations.
*Deor* is a poet talking about the inevitablity of sorrow and death, but the impermanence of suffering nonetheless.

*Wulf and Eadwacer* seems to be about a lost lover named Wulf and then there’s also a guy named Eadwacer? Not exactly clear to me, but it seems that Wulf has taken a child away?

*The Wife’s Lament* is about a wife who is taken by her husband far from their people and family, to a place with few others. Thankfully, in that place, she meets someone whom she finds a closeness and friendship with. Then her husband, presumably out of jealousy, makes her live in a cave under a tree, where she mourns the loss of her friend. 

## **Scenario for The Wife’s Lament**
For fun, we’ll call the narrator Brynhild and her husband Sigurd. 
	Before the events of this story, Brynhild was a kind and loving member of her community, the type to bring fresh baked bread to her in-laws just because she felt like doing something nice. Sigurd, on the other hand, is power-hungry and self-centered. Sigurd has failed to seize political power in his homeland but has been allowed to live. He cannot remain among his friends and family in that land, of course, because to do so might give him the opportunity to attempt another coup. He and his household are declared outlaws and must flee from their ancestral lands. In their exile, Sigurd and Brynhild separate intentionally to avoid being separated by their enemies. Sigurd tells Brynhild to go off to some remote place and wait for him, and she does so. I imagine that this place is some incredibly rocky, windswept and seagull-covered rock with a few miserable folks eking out a miserable existence. 
	While she remains on this island, incredibly lonely, Brynhild meets another man, and they bond over their shared misery at being stuck there. Then, there is some ambiguity as to whether or not Sigurd meets her on the island, or if someone else in power makes the decision to imprison her. There is a definite air of jealousy in the abruptness of her transition from friendship to isolation, as though someone has arrived and decided immediately to intervene. Regardless, the wife is condemned to life beneath the earth (though I do not think she is killed) and lives out her days in solitude. Her torment comes at the hands of the cruel world she lives in, where her love and kindness, her need for even just platonic relationships, result in solitude and misery.
