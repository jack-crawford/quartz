## Basics
Thomas More’s *Utopia* (pun on *eutopia* and *outopia*, both happy-place and no-place) was originally written in Latin, and published about 25 years after Columbus returned from America. 

The fundamental structure of it is capturing a tale from a fictional explorer, a former member of Amerigo Vespucci’s crew who stayed abroad and lived with the Utopians before making his way back to Europe. He recounts his experiences with these better societies and specifically with the Utopians to More. Book One sets the stage, includes some retold dialogues between the explorer and some Englishmen who refuse to see the merits of the systems he suggests. Book Two is the actual account of Utopia. 

## Big takeaways 
Utopia is a place without private property, without need, and thus without greed. There are communist levels to it — everyone dresses the same, everyone eats the same food (though the best cuts are served to the elders among each set of households), and everyone takes their turn in farm work. 

Parts of this book are very radical, and parts are very much of their time. For all the good parts of the country, the island was colonized by the architects of the scheme, and the natives who resisted the society have been driven out or enslaved. 

Pleasure is the great priority of the Utopians, though to decrease your own pleasure for another’s gain is a virtue. Impinging on another’s pleasure to further your own is a great sin. 

Though the Utopians have many cults, their supreme god is Mithra, but eventually they adopt Christ pretty readily. They have very strong religious tolerance. 

In wartime, they pursue an aggressive strategic tactic - hiring armies of mercenaries and incentivizing assassination and usurpation in the enemy nation. They only fight themselves as a last resort. Also medical suicide is encouraged in terminally ill people! Yay!

“This is why Plato in a very fine comparison declares that wise men are right in keeping clear of public business. They see the people swarming through the streets and getting soaked with rain, and they cannot persuade them to go indoors and get out of the wet. They know if they go out themselves, they can do no good but only get drenched with the rest. So they stay indoors and are content to at least themselves dry, since they cannot remedy the folly of others” (35).

“The other hours of the day, when they are not working, eating, or sleeping, are left to each person’s individual discretion, provided that free time is not wasted in roistering or sloth but used properly in some chosen occupation. Generally these periods are devoted to intellectual activity. For they have an established custom of giving daily public lectures before dawn; attendance at these lectures is required only of those who have been specially chosen to devote themselves to learning, but a great many other people, both men and women, choose voluntarily to attend” (45-46).

“For where money is the standard of everything, many vain, superfluous trades are bound to be carried on simply to satisfy luxury and licentiousness” (47). 

“Even more than this, the Utopians are appalled at those people who practically worship a rich man, though they neither owe him anything more are obligated to him in any way. What impresses them is simply that the man is rich. yet all the while they know he is so mean and grasping that as long as he lives not a single penny out of that great mound of money will ever come their way” (58). 

“They define virtue as living according to nature, and God, they say, created us to that end” (60).

“They think that the contemplation of nature, and the sense of reverence arising from it, are acts of worship to God” (88). 

## Quotes from Commentary
### C.S. Lewis
“The interpretation which breaks down soonest is the ‘liberal’ interpretation… There is no freedom of speech in Utopia” (199).  

“It is not love of liberty that makes men write Utopias” (199).

“According to [R.W. Chambers] the Utopians represent the natural virtues working at their ideal best in isolation from the theological” (200).

“These puzzles may give the impression that the *Utopia* is a confused book: and if it were intended as a serious treatise it would be very confused indeed. *On my view, however, it appears confused only so long as we are trying to get out of it what it never intended to give.* It becomes intelligible and delightful as soon as we take it for what it is— a holiday work… a revel of debate. … Its place on our shelves is close to *Gulliver*… a long way from the *Republic* or *New Worlds for Old*”(200-201).
