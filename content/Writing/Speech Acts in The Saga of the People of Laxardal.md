Speech acts ran Norse society in the 13th century. For a culture spanning five or six countries (depending on how you want to count the now-United Kingdom) to survive and thrive in the era of slow, dangerous sea travel, honorable speech and individual truth were absolutely essential. The relay of communication and verificative response could take months or, depending on the time of year, entire seasons. The results of an order given may not be found out for a long time, if at all, so the people who commit themselves to carrying out the order had to be trusted entirely. Words carried weight, and if something was said (before enough people) it would be done. *The Saga of the People of Laxardal* is full of these sorts of declarations which actually drive the plot along further than physical acts. The death of Kjartan is an exciting battle, for example, but the aggregated speech acts that led to that unavoidable confrontation are almost more meaningful than the actual swings of his soft-metal sword. 

 There is a very small speech act that carries a great deal of weight in Kjartan’s story, when he first encounters King Olaf Tryggvason. Kjartan and Bolli have traveled to the mainland to make something of themselves, and stumble upon a group of men swimming. Kjartan sees one of them is stronger than the rest, and decides to challenge him. This challenge takes the form of an attempted drowning, which must be a cultural difference that I don’t quite relate to, but he and his opponent are evenly matched and swim to shore. The stranger asks after Kjartan’s name, his other skills, and finally, after Kjartan’s sparse answers, asks why he hasn’t been asked anything in return. 
“The man spoke: ‘It does make a difference who your opponent is; why haven’t you asked me any questions?’
Kjartan replied, **‘I don’t care who you are.’**” (87). 

This is a bold speech act, just six words packed to the brim with implication. Kjartan represents himself as fully independent, a man who has just arrived in the complicated political world of Norway, agreed with his fellow Icelanders to reject the king’s religious mandate, and is now willing to face any man in sport or combat. He respects his opponent’s skill, but identity and title make no difference. From a lesser man, this bold approach could have led to a prompt execution, but King Olaf responds with respect and goes on to introduce himself. “You’re not only highly capable, but highly confident of yourself as well” (87). Kjartan then backs up speech action with physical action. “Kjartan made no answer but turned to leave without putting on his outer cloak. He was wearing an inner shirt of scarlet. By this time the king was practically fully dressed. He called out to Kjartan, asking him not to hurry off, and Kjartan turned back reluctantly, The king then removed a fine cloak from his own shoulders and gave it to Kjartan, saying it wouldn’t do for him to return to his men without a cloak” (87). Kjartan’s barely-polite aloofness, declared aloud first and foremost, sets the tone for his entire relationship with the king, one which develops into one of mutual fondness and respect of the highest level.
